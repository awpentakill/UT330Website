Two labs: a parks site and a personal profile

September 11, 2026 · Week 2

This entry catches up on the last two weeks: the vocabulary from the September 4 lecture, and two hands-on labs I built from starter files — a mock city parks-and-recreation website, and a personal profile page in the old-web “about me” tradition.

Structure, skeleton, surface

The September 4 class, Structure Is Interaction: Wayfinding Ep. 2 (Ron Bronson), is the lecture behind the wayfinding walk I wrote up separately. Before that walk, though, we went through a set of terms for describing how a person gets from wanting something to getting it: goal → input/action → system response → feedback → next action.

Affordance
What an object or environment makes possible for a particular person.
Signifier
The visible cue that tells someone an action is possible.
Control
The thing a person operates to cause a change.
Constraint
What limits the available actions.
Feedback
What the system returns after an action.
State
The current condition of the system, which decides what can happen next.

Zooming out, a site has a structure (what exists and what comes first), a skeleton (where content, controls, and navigation sit), and a surface (color, type, styling) — on top of information-architecture terms for organizing it: hierarchy, taxonomy, label, navigation, sequence, and progressive disclosure. The in-class exercise was diagnosing friction in an existing site: the effort between intent and completion.

Types of friction we practiced naming
Type What it means
Label The words don’t match what’s behind them.
Hierarchy The important thing isn’t the prominent thing.
Sequence The right information, in the wrong order.
Buried It’s there, but nobody will find it.
Missing The site assumes you already know.

Lab: Riverton Parks & Recreation

The first lab was a starter template for a fictional city parks department — Home, Parks, Reservations, and Rules & Contact pages, styled like an old municipal site with a navy header, beige “window” panels, and a visitor counter — that I filled in with the department’s real content: hours, fees, and permit rules for three parks. I renamed the parks along the way; Naidan Park, Bursley Park, and the GGB Sports Complex are all UMich buildings wearing a different hat. Nothing about the underlying content changed, but the page reads like mine now, which was a good demonstration of how much work a label is doing.

The Reservations page reuses the same fee and permit information as a table, with a <details> element for “Before you apply” — a small case of progressive disclosure, since most visitors don’t need the special-event or alcohol-permit rules and shouldn’t have to scroll past them. I also noticed the sports field rate is listed twice, once for youth nonprofits and once for everyone else, which reads like a duplicated row until you actually check the fee column.

Lab: a personal profile page

The second lab was a personal “profile” starter, closer to an old social-profile page than a portfolio: a photo panel, a status line, a details table, a visitor counter, a Now Playing embed, and a Top Friends grid. I personalized the home page — name, location, program, contact buttons, mood/listening/working-on details, and a Spotify track in the Now Playing slot — but only got as far as the home page. About, Blog, and Friends still show the starter’s placeholder text.

The two labs used the same vocabulary from opposite directions. Riverton Parks is about making unfamiliar civic content easy to scan and act on for a stranger. The profile page is about making familiar personal content recognizably mine for someone who already knows me. Same structure, skeleton, and surface; different job.

Vocabulary from class

  • Affordance / signifier: the parks site’s nav tabs and the profile’s contact buttons both say what they do before you click them.
  • Label: renaming the parks changed nothing structurally and changed how the whole page feels.
  • Progressive disclosure: the “Before you apply” details on the Reservations page, collapsed until someone needs it.
