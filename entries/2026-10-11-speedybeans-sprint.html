Team sprint: SpeedyBeans order status and pickup

October 11, 2026 · Week 6

For our product team interaction design sprint, five of us designed a mobile pre-order app for a fictional coffee shop, SpeedyBeans. The brief was for people on their way to work: a regular customer should be able to finish an order in about sixty seconds and then pick it up without having to think. Each of us owned one screen, and I had the last one, Screen 5: order status and pickup. This entry goes through how that screen changed over three phases, and what I think worked and didn’t across the team.

How we split the work

We worked one screen per person in a shared Figma file, then critiqued each other’s screens and negotiated until the five of them read as one app. Then we revised together.

Phase 1: a greyscale sketch

I started with a low-fidelity wireframe in greys only, using the same font as the final version so the type hierarchy would carry forward. The point was to settle structure before surface: what someone waiting for a coffee needs to see, and in what order. That meant a status box at the top, a three-step tracker, a placeholder for a “ready” notice, the order number, the drink, and where to pick it up.

Next to the sketch I left notes on what each piece should become in the final version:

One status box
Becomes three states (placed, being made, ready) that each change colour and icon.
Stepper works
Keep the three steps, but add Done / Now / Up next text so it doesn’t rely on colour alone.
Ready notice is a dashed box
Becomes a solid banner: “Your coffee is ready! Pick up #128 at the counter,” plus a phone alert.
#128 bigger
The barista calls out the number, so it has to be the easiest thing on the screen to read.
Where do I go?
The store and the exact spot belong together in one card.

Phase 2: three states in Material 3

Phase 2 turned the sketch into a finished screen built from the Material 3 design kit, with one screen for each order state. Most of the work was in the feedback: each state answers the question someone has at that moment. Did it go through? How long now? Where do I go? The wait time counts down (“about 4 minutes,” then “about 2”). The tracker labels every step. When the drink is ready, the whole top card flips to a solid purple banner and the pickup card is highlighted too.

I made the status card, the tracker, the order details, and the pickup info into reusable components. The three states are the same screen with different states switched on, and that paid off in the next phase.

Phase 3: someone else picks it up

The change order was that customers increasingly order on the way to work but ask a coworker, partner, or friend to pick it up. As a team we had to agree on four things. When does the customer make that choice? What does the shop actually need? What does the pickup person need to know? Does the confirmation change?

What we decided for the pickup-person change
Question Our answer
When? Before payment, with “Myself” or “Someone else.” It can still be changed later from my screen.
What the shop needs A name, a phone number for the ready text, and the order number. No payment details and no address.
What the pickup person needs One text with the order number, the store, the exact spot, and the name to give at the counter.
Confirmation Names who is picking up and offers a way to share the details.

On my screen, the change came down to one new component: a Pickup person card with the name, a masked phone number, a Change link, and a preview of the exact text the other person gets. The button became “Send pickup details to Jordan.” Because Phase 2 was built from components, nothing else on the screen had to be redrawn.

What went well

  • Splitting one screen per person meant everyone owned a piece and nothing was designed twice.
  • Using the same Material 3 kit and palette made five people’s screens look like one app.
  • Sketching in greyscale first caught hierarchy problems early, like the order number not standing out, before any colour was involved.
  • Building with components made the Phase 3 change cheap. I added one card instead of redoing the screen.
  • The pickup-person flow runs across Screens 1, 4, and 5, so the change order reads as one connected flow, not five separate patches.

What could have been better

  • Shared sample data. Put side by side, our screens disagree on the order number (#128, #941, SB-2048), the store (5th & Main vs. Central Campus), the drink, and even the pickup person (Jordan Lee on mine, Alex Chen elsewhere). We should have agreed on one example order on day one.
  • One rule for pickup data. Some screens ask for only a name, and others ask for a name and phone number. That’s a real decision we never wrote down.
  • Frame sizes. My screens are 412 × 1072, and others are 390 × 844, which made lining them up for the presentation awkward.
  • No real users. We critiqued each other, but nobody tested whether the ready banner gets noticed or whether a regular can actually finish an order in sixty seconds.

What I would do next. Hand someone a phone showing the Being made state and ask them to tell me where to go and what to say at the counter. If they can’t answer in a few seconds, the pickup card isn’t doing its job yet.

Vocabulary from class

  • Feedback: each order state is the system’s answer to “what’s happening with my coffee?”
  • State: one screen with three states rather than three separate screens.
  • Hierarchy: #128 is the biggest thing on the screen because it’s the thing the barista says out loud.
  • Structure, skeleton, surface: the sketch settled structure and skeleton, and Phase 2 was mostly surface.
