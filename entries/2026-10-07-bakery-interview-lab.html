Design research lab: interviewing a bakery

October 7, 2026 · Week 6

The Design Research Methods lab gave us a scenario: a neighborhood bakery is wondering whether it could stay open later without making its current staff work longer days. Our job wasn’t to answer that question. It was to write interview guides that would help us find out what actually decides the bakery’s hours. I wrote two guides, one for the bakery side and one for a customer. Then I practiced both on an AI playing each role.

The guide structure

The lab document gave a five-part structure for a 15-minute interview. I used Codex, an AI assistant, to draft the first guide from the lab document, then edited it into my own version.

The five parts of the bakery interview guide
Part Time What it’s for
Introduction 1 min Who I am, why I’m asking, permission to take notes, and that they can skip anything.
Warm up 2 min Easy, factual questions: what they do, and what yesterday’s hours were.
Stories 9 min The core: “tell me about the last time…” questions about real events.
Show me 2 min They walk me through checking tomorrow’s schedule on their phone.
Close 1 min “Is there anything I should have asked?”

The main lesson was in how the questions are worded. Almost every question asks about a specific thing that already happened: “Walk me through yesterday,” “Tell me about the last time you decided to keep or change the hours,” “Walk me through a recent closing.” None of them ask “Would you stay open later?” or “What do you think about evening hours?” People are bad at predicting what they’d do and good at remembering what they did.

Practice interview 1: Luis, a baker

I asked the AI to play someone who works at the bakery, not a consultant. It had to answer only what I asked, could be uncertain or wrong, and shouldn’t explain the whole problem. It became Luis, a baker who works 4 a.m. to noon in a shop open 7 to 2.

The first thing the practice run caught was a mistake in my guide. The header still said Participant: Bakery Owner, but I was interviewing a baker, and several questions only made sense for an owner. Luis said as much:

I don’t decide the opening hours. The owner asked me a few weeks ago what staying open until six would mean for us.

Luis, simulated baker (AI role-play), October 7

His answer to my cost-and-sales question was basically “I don’t see those numbers.” That question belonged in the owner’s interview. The guide needs a different version for each person, not one guide with the name swapped.

What the story questions did get out of him was more useful than I expected:

“Later” means two different things
Fresh bread in the afternoon and the counter staying open longer are “pretty different days” for a baker. The question “should we stay open later?” hides that split.
Food is left over at closing
At closing there were country loaves and a lot of muffins left, some set aside for a discount shelf the next day.
Schedules live in a group chat
The rota is a photo in the staff chat. If an order changes after he goes home and nobody posts it, he finds out at 4 a.m.
Hours aren’t only a business constraint
He picks up his daughter in the afternoon, so he can’t just stay late when a batch runs over. Consistency matters to him more than length.

Practice interview 2: Emma, a customer

Next I wrote a shorter guide for a customer, with six questions in the same style: the most recent bakery visit, the last time you wanted baked goods later in the day, a time the hours didn’t fit, the most recent afternoon or evening purchase, ordering ahead, and a show-me on the phone. This time I gave the AI a persona: Emma, a mechanical engineering junior whose sleep schedule is all over the place.

  • On Sunday she woke up at three, wanted something savory around five, and found the bakery closed. She checked a delivery app, decided the fees weren’t worth it for one item, and made frozen dumplings.
  • Her phone tells her whether a bakery is open, but not what’s left. She wouldn’t call to ask about one croissant. She’d just go somewhere else.
  • She’d order ahead for a birthday, but not for a snack, because she doesn’t know what she wants until she’s hungry.
  • Her last line changed how I read the whole scenario: when she says “late,” she often means five in the afternoon, not midnight.

Put next to Luis’s interview, there’s a tension worth checking with real people. The bakery has muffins left over at two, and a customer like Emma wants exactly that kind of thing at five but has no way of knowing it’s there. I’m not treating that as a finding, because both of these people were invented.

What I took from it

  • Story questions work. “Tell me about the last time” got concrete details like times, prices, and what happened next, and none of the predictions you get from “would you…?”
  • Write one guide per role. A baker, an owner, and a customer know different things. Asking a baker about payroll wastes two of the fifteen minutes.
  • Watch the wording. My pasted version of the guide had awkward phrasing, like “Take me to review yesterday.” If a question is hard to read, it’s harder to say out loud to a stranger.
  • The closing question earns its minute. Both “anything else?” answers brought up something I hadn’t asked about: the invisible next-day prep, and what “late” actually means.

The limits of practicing on an AI. It was a good rehearsal. It caught a wrong participant label and a question that belonged in another interview. But Emma craved food after hours because I told her to. A simulated participant answers in complete, tidy stories, never goes off topic, and tends to confirm whatever the persona was built around. It tests the guide, not the bakery. The real version needs real bakers and real customers.

Vocabulary from class

  • Story-based questions: ask about the last real instance, not opinions or predictions.
  • Probe: “What happened next?” and “Can you give an example?”, used only when there’s time.
  • Show me: watching someone do the task on their own phone, which surfaced the group-chat rota and the “open now” search.
  • Constraint: a 4 a.m. start and a school pickup shape the bakery’s hours as much as sales do.
